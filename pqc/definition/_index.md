**Guidance** — Strategy and vision form the foundation for quantum-safe transition. Without executive sponsorship recognizing the quantum threat, PQC migration will lack resources and priority. The Dutch Migration Handbook identifies organizational personas ranging from 'unaware' to 'frontrunner,' with most European organizations currently in early awareness stages. Strategic planning must address the Mosca inequality: if data sensitivity lifespan plus migration time exceeds time until cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQC), action is required now. The criteria apply to certificate issuers (CA/TSP) and to organizations that build PKI-dependent products. For a software vendor, the strategic question is whether PQC enters the product roadmap and customer commitments, not just the internal certificate estate.

**Guidance** — RFC 3647 governs Certificate Policy and CPS structure. PQC migration requires fundamental policy updates: algorithm specifications must expand to include NIST FIPS 203/204/205 (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA), deprecation schedules must align with SP 800-131A Rev 3 and regional requirements, hybrid vs pure PQC strategy must be documented, and Cryptographic Bill of Materials (CBOM) requirements are emerging as compliance necessity. Multi-jurisdiction complexity creates policy challenges: European regulators (BSI, ANSSI) require hybrid signatures during transition, while CNSA 2.0 recommends pure PQ for US National Security Systems. Key management policies must address larger key sizes, HSM PQC support requirements, and stateful hash-based signature state management where applicable. These criteria are written for a certificate issuer maintaining CP/CPS. An organization that only consumes certificates should read them as requirements to place on its providers: require PQC provisions, deprecation schedules, and CBOM support from CA and trust-service suppliers, and check them through procurement and contracts rather than writing the policy itself.

**Guidance** — The regulatory landscape for PQC is evolving rapidly with binding requirements now in effect. DORA establishes comprehensive ICT risk management requirements for EU financial entities from January 2025, creating regulatory pressure for cryptographic resilience. The EU Coordinated Roadmap recommends PQC preparations by 2026 with critical infrastructure protection by 2030. The 18 EU Member States Joint Statement urges prioritization and roadmap development for PQC transition. **Guidance** — The transition to post-quantum cryptography transforms operational processes fundamentally. This is not merely an algorithm swap; it represents a multi-year major programme requiring new procedures across every PKI operational domain. Key process considerations include: key ceremony evolution for PQC algorithms (larger keys, hybrid generation, stateful HBS state management), crypto-agility procedures per NIST CSWP 39, quantum risk assessment integration using the Mosca inequality framework, operational change management for phased migration, incident response for cryptographic events, and extended segregation of duties for PQC-specific roles. Segregation of duties must extend to PQC-specific roles so that algorithm selection, implementation, and validation are not concentrated in one person. The party that approves which algorithms enter the trust store should not be the same party that implements them, and independent validation should confirm the implementation matches the approved cryptographic policy. This mirrors the established CA separation between policy authority and operations, applied to cryptographic agility.

**Guidance** — The Cryptography category records the organization's cryptographic choices for the PQC transition: which algorithms and protocols are allowed and in which combinations, where cryptography is in use, when it must be retired, and how cryptographic change is governed. Algorithm governance means a written position on three groups of standards: the final PQC standards, ML-KEM (FIPS 203), ML-DSA (FIPS 204), and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), finalized in August 2024; emerging standards such as HQC and FN-DSA, selected but not yet finalized; and, where applicable, the stateful hash-based signature schemes of NIST SP 800-208. A useful position names the algorithm and parameter set, what it may be used for, and whether it must run in hybrid mode alongside classical cryptography or may run alone, recorded separately for key establishment and for digital signatures because the answers often differ. National authority positions on hybrid deployment range from recommended, to mandatory within regulated scopes, to not required; record which authorities apply and with what force, and check the referenced BSI, ANSSI, and NSA sources for the current positions. Not all external deprecation deadlines carry the same force: record whether each is legally binding, advisory, or still a draft, and set internal dates accordingly. Visibility underpins all of this: a maintained inventory, such as a cryptographic bill of materials (CBOM) or a documented equivalent identifying algorithms, protocols, and where they are deployed, is what makes prioritization, impact analysis, and completion checks possible. The referenced NIST white paper on crypto agility surveys strategies and practices for keeping algorithm change possible. These levels add a PQC-specific reading on top of the base Cryptography category and its six requirements; they replace nothing. They govern cryptography rather than operate it: key and certificate operations belong to the Management module, and operational records appear here only as evidence that a governance decision was made and followed. Where a provider operates the cryptography, evidence can come from contracts, provider confirmations, and product documentation or CBOMs, with remaining gaps noted; a certificate-consuming organization places these requirements on its providers through procurement. For a software vendor, governance defines permitted product defaults and customer commitments; whether shipped products conform is assessed elsewhere in the model.

**Assessment** — Key Probes: - Which post-quantum algorithms has the organization taken a documented decision on, and where is each decision recorded, with its parameters and permitted uses? - Are deployment choices recorded separately for key establishment and for digital signatures, and do they reference the authority positions that shaped them? - Which systems does the cryptographic inventory cover, which are excluded, and where are known blind spots written down? - Which external transition deadlines apply, and is each recorded as binding, advisory, or draft? - Has an algorithm change ever been taken through the governance process, in production or as a planned exercise, and what changed as a result? Warning Signs: - Approval documentation takes no position on post-quantum algorithms - Algorithms are approved on paper, but no one can map them to the systems, services, or providers that actually use them - Inventory work tries to find everything before prioritizing anything, delaying usable transition plans - Deprecation rules without dates, or dates that do not distinguish binding requirements from advisory guidance and drafts - Crypto-agility claimed in policy, with no completed change or exercise to show for it Evidence Examples by Level: - Level 1: No documented position on post-quantum algorithms - Level 2: A recorded first assessment of which final PQC standards apply; a starting inventory for the highest-risk systems - Level 3: A decision register with parameters and permitted uses; an inventory covering the agreed scope; deprecation dates with their status noted; approved protocol rules including permitted fallbacks - Level 4: Review records with a set rhythm and defined triggers; inventory coverage measured against an agreed system list; records showing non-compliant usage was resolved - Level 5: Records of a completed change or exercise with findings and follow-up actions; a tracking list for standards still in development, with decision points
